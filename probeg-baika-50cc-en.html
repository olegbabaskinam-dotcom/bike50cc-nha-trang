Buying a used bike

What mileage is normal for a 50cc bike

In a listing, mileage often looks like the main argument: “only 3,000 km”, “hardly ridden”, “kept in a garage”. It's a nice signal, but not a guarantee. For a used bike, what matters more is how alive it is now: how it starts, brakes, holds the road and looks in the details. The odometer is the start of the conversation, not a reason to send money right away.

There is no single “right” number

Low mileage on a 50cc is good, but only if it matches the age and the condition. A bike with 5,000 km, worn grips, bald tyres and tired brakes raises more questions than a neat bike with 15,000 km and a clear service history.

So we don't split listings into “under 10,000 — buy” and “over — no”. We look at the combination: year, price, mileage, the state of the plastics, wheels, brakes and the sound of the engine. The lower the stated mileage on an old bike, the more calmly and carefully it needs checking.

Five things that must match the mileage

  1. Tyres and wheels. Look at the tread, cracks on the sidewalls and the rims. The tyres don't have to be original, but their condition should fit the seller's story.
  2. Grips, buttons and footrests. Daily use shows up on them quickly. Heavily worn parts on a “minimal mileage” bike are a reason to ask why.
  3. Brakes. Ask for a video of calm braking. The levers shouldn't sink, and the bike shouldn't pull to the side or squeal as if it urgently needs repair.
  4. Cold start. You need to see the very first start, not an already warm engine. Let the seller show a short uncut video: start, idle, pull away and ride.
  5. Plastics and fasteners. Small scratches are normal for a city bike. But fresh paint on one spot, uneven gaps or signs of a hard fall are questions about the bike's history, not its mileage.

What to ask the seller

No interrogation is needed. Just ask: who rode the bike, why it's being sold, when the oil was changed, what work has been done and whether there are any issues now. A normal seller usually answers directly. If the numbers and the story keep changing, don't waste time on this bike.

When buying remotely we ask for the same as always: a photo of the registration showing the plate number and 49.5/50cc, plus a live video of a start-up and a short ride. How to check the engine size by the papers — in this article.

When mileage is a stop sign

High mileage alone doesn't make a bike bad. The stop sign is when the mileage looks implausible or the seller won't confirm the condition on video. We walk away if he avoids a cold start, shows the bike only from a distance, can't explain obvious wear or asks for a prepayment before a proper check.

How we look at it when buying

We don't buy the number on the dash. First we check the papers and the real engine size, then the mechanics and the looks. After buying we do basic servicing, washing and preparation — so the buyer in Nha Trang gets a clear bike, not someone else's problem under a nice photo.
